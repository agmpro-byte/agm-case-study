About $80,000 owed on service jobs we'd already finished.

Heavenly Greens' service department ran the old way: paper tickets, appointments in Salesforce, card forms through DocuSign and cards charged by hand. Nothing followed up, nothing was automated, and no dashboard showed what was owed. By August, about $80,000 of finished service work had never been collected. This is how we rebuilt the department so a finished job can't go unpaid.

The client

The department nobody was watching

Heavenly Greens' service department handles everything after the install: repairs, warranty visits, the call that says something isn't right. Sales and installs had the dashboards and the attention. Service ran on whatever was left over.

That is where the money went quiet.

The problem
Why was finished service work going unpaid?

Because every step of the department lived somewhere different, and none of them talked to each other:

  • Tickets and crew notes on paper. When a visit ended, the paperwork was often the last anyone saw of it.
  • Appointments in Salesforce. The service calendar had never been moved anywhere else, so service visits kept being booked there.
  • Cards collected the old way. A card authorization form went out through DocuSign, the card was charged by hand in a separate processor, and the payment was typed back into Salesforce.
  • Invoices in QuickBooks, a fourth place, with nothing connecting it to the job that was finished.

No step followed up on the one before it. Nothing was automated. No dashboard showed what was owed, only what was billed. The department was working hard and running blind.

The investigation

Three answers for the same money

On August 5 we asked a simple question: how much has the service department collected this year? Three systems gave three answers.

Where we lookedWhat it saidWhat it was really counting
Salesforce service dashboard$123,090Billed, not collected
Payments typed into Salesforce$121,724Whatever someone remembered to enter
QuickBooks$103,445Cash that actually arrived

About $18,000 apart, and not one of them could say whether a given finished job had been paid in full. On August 12, Troy put a number on what was falling through the gaps: about $80,000 of finished service work, never collected. Not lost, not disputed. Just nobody asking for it.

The system's own first count, on November 4, found 41 finished jobs not paid in full. The oldest had waited 581 days. That is a different count from the estimate, made a different way, and both pointed at the same broken loop.

What we built

Rebuilding the department, one step at a time

August 10

One place for every service ticket

A service portal and ticket registry, born from a single complaint email. Customers send photos and video; every ticket lives in one record instead of on paper.

August 27

The first ticket runs the whole way

Intake, photos, pricing, booking, crew, and an invoice with a pay link and a scan-to-pay code, all on one rail, with no paper and no hand-typed payment.

October 15

The whole department, retooled

Six roles and seven lanes of work, each with an owner, so nothing sits between two people.

October 30

Service leaves Salesforce, and the board says collect

Service appointments stop running through Salesforce, and the service board gets its first Collect payment button.

November 4

The first count

41 finished jobs not paid in full; the oldest 581 days.

November 6

Three steps from finished to paid

Invoices to send, collect payment, then the call center. Nothing finished drops out of view until it is paid in full.

The top of the service board now has one button per kind of money waiting, each with a count and how long the oldest has waited:

  • Invoices to send: the work is done and the customer hasn't been invoiced.
  • Collect payment: the invoice is out and the money isn't in, with a tracker showing whether the customer opened it. After 7 days the row turns red.
  • Call about the unpaid invoice: at that point the call center gets a line on its own list to call the customer.

Why this matters

The department after the sale is where money goes quiet

For the owner

You paid for the materials, the crew and the trip. Every unpaid service job is a loan you never agreed to make.

For accounting

The books are right about what was invoiced. They can't tell you what was never invoiced at all.

For the crew

Finished means finished in the field. Nobody in the field owns whether it got paid.

For cash flow

The older a balance gets, the harder it is to collect. 581 days is a very old balance.

Try this in your business this week

Audit your service department this week

  1. List every service job you finished in the last two years.
  2. Next to each: invoiced? paid in full?
  3. Count how many systems it took to answer. Every extra one is a place money can hide.
  4. Sort by days since it was finished. Anything unpaid after 30 days gets a phone call this week.
